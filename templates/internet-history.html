Introduction

The Internet is a worldwide network that allows computers and devices to communicate with each other. It developed gradually from early networking research such as ARPANET. Technologies such as packet switching and TCP/IP made it possible to connect different networks together. Over time, the Internet expanded from a research network into the global communication system used today.

Verified Milestones

1967 — ARPANET Planning

In 1967, Larry Roberts developed and published a plan for ARPANET while working with DARPA. This was important because it moved packet-switching networking from theory toward a practical research network connecting different institutions.

Source: Internet Society — A Brief History of the Internet

1969 — ARPANET Begins

In 1969, ARPANET began with four computer nodes, and the first computer-to-computer signal was sent between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute on October 29. This demonstrated that computers at different locations could successfully communicate through a packet-switched network.

Source: DARPA — ARPANET

1971–1972 — Network Email Develops

Ray Tomlinson developed network email and introduced the use of the "@" symbol to separate the user name from the destination address. Email quickly became one of the earliest and most widely used applications on computer networks.

Source: Internet Hall of Fame — Raymond Tomlinson

1973 — Internetworking Research Begins

DARPA began research into connecting different packet-switched networks. This was essential because it introduced the idea of connecting independent networks into a larger "network of networks," which became a central principle of the Internet.

Source: Internet Society

1974 — TCP Design

Robert Kahn and Vinton Cerf developed the early design of the Transmission Control Protocol. Their work made it possible for different computer networks to communicate with one another and helped establish the concept of an interconnected "network of networks."

Source: ETHW — TCP Enables the Internet, 1974

1983 — ARPANET Switches to TCP/IP

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET completed its transition from NCP to TCP/IP. TCP/IP allowed different networks and computers to communicate using a common protocol suite and became the technical foundation of the modern Internet.

Source: Internet Society

1986 — NSFNET Expands Academic Networking

The U.S. National Science Foundation developed NSFNET to connect researchers and supercomputer centers. NSFNET became an important high-speed backbone and helped expand Internet access throughout universities and research institutions.

Source: U.S. National Science Foundation

1990 — ARPANET Is Decommissioned

ARPANET was shut down in 1990. By this time, TCP/IP networking had expanded far beyond the original ARPANET, showing that the Internet had developed into a much larger interconnected system.

Source: Internet Society

1995 — NSFNET Backbone Transition

The NSFNET backbone service ended as commercial and privately operated Internet networks expanded. This represented an important transition toward the commercial Internet infrastructure used by the public.

Source: U.S. National Science Foundation

1997 — Wi-Fi Standard

In 1997, the IEEE 802.11 wireless networking standard was published. Wi-Fi made it possible for devices to connect to networks without physical cables and eventually made home and mobile Internet access much more convenient.

Source: IEEE — Evolution of Wi-Fi

1998 — ICANN Is Formed

ICANN was formed in 1998 to coordinate important Internet identifiers such as domain names and related numbering systems. It provided a new international structure for managing critical Internet resources as the network continued to expand globally.

Source: ICANN — About ICANN

2001 — 3G Expands Mobile Internet

In October 2001, NTT DOCOMO launched its fully commercial FOMA service in Japan, using the W-CDMA 3G standard. This was an important step in mobile Internet development because cellular networks could carry richer data services, helping people connect while away from a fixed Internet connection.

Source: NTT DOCOMO — History

2004 — Broadband Internet Adoption Grows

By August 2004, broadband subscriptions in OECD countries had passed 100 million. Broadband connections, including DSL and cable, offered faster access than dial-up. Their growing adoption made high-speed Internet more widely available to homes and businesses and supported larger amounts of data moving across networks.

Source: OECD — Communications Outlook 2005 (Chapter 5, PDF)

2009 — Commercial 4G/LTE Arrives

In December 2009, TeliaSonera launched a commercial LTE network in Stockholm with Ericsson. LTE, commonly called 4G, brought faster mobile broadband to meet growing demand from smartphones. This helped mobile networks carry more Internet traffic and made high-speed connectivity practical beyond fixed broadband lines.

Source: Ericsson — 4G Arrives (December 2009 Launch)

2012 — World IPv6 Launch

On June 6, 2012, participating Internet service providers, home router manufacturers, and online services permanently enabled IPv6 through World IPv6 Launch. IPv6 provides a much larger address space than IPv4. Its deployment helped the Internet keep growing as more people and devices needed network addresses.

Source: Internet Society — World IPv6 Launch

2021 — ITU Publishes Global 5G Specifications

In February 2021, ITU published the first edition of its IMT-2020 specifications for 5G. These global standards supported the development of mobile networks designed for faster data transfers, shorter delays, and many connected devices. This expanded the possibilities for mobile Internet access and the Internet of Things.

Source: ITU — 5G Networks

2025 — Modern Internet Connectivity Reaches Six Billion People

ITU estimated that six billion people used the Internet in 2025 and that 5G networks covered 55% of the world's population. This shows how modern Internet connectivity has grown into a global infrastructure. However, 2.2 billion people remained offline, making wider coverage and affordable, reliable access important priorities for continued Internet development.

Source: ITU — Facts and Figures 2025

Milestone Summary

These milestones are supported by the Internet Society and the U.S. National Science Foundation. The Internet Society documents the first ARPANET connections in 1969, the public demonstration of ARPANET and the growth of email in 1972, and the transition to TCP/IP on January 1, 1983. The National Science Foundation explains that NSFNET expanded academic networking and later became an important backbone of the growing Internet before commercial networks took over during the 1990s.

Three Key Internet Technologies

1. Packet Switching

Packet switching divides data into smaller pieces called packets. These packets can travel through a network and are reassembled when they reach their destination. This approach was fundamental to early computer networking and ARPANET.

2. TCP/IP

TCP/IP refers to the Transmission Control Protocol and Internet Protocol, which together form the foundation of the Internet Protocol suite. TCP helps manage reliable communication between computers, while IP handles addressing and forwarding packets between networks. TCP/IP was essential because it allowed different networks to communicate with each other. The Internet Society documents the transition of ARPANET to TCP/IP on January 1, 1983.

3. Routers

Routers connect different computer networks and decide where packets should be sent. Early Internet research referred to these connecting devices as gateways; they later became known as routers.
